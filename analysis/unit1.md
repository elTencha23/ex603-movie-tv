# Unit 1 — Modeling Justification

Design rationale for the movies database schema

## Primary Key Choices

Every table uses a surrogate primary key — an auto\-incrementing integer (`user_id`, `movie_id`, `rating_id`, `genre_id`, `movie_genre_id`) generated by PostgreSQL's `SERIAL` type — rather than a natural key drawn from the data itself, such as `email` or `genre_name`. Natural keys are tempting because they carry meaning, but they are riskier as identifiers: an email address can change, and a title or genre name is not guaranteed to stay unique or immutable over the life of the system. Surrogate keys avoid that fragility entirely — they never change, they are compact for indexing and joins, and they keep the meaning of a row separate from its identity. `email` and `genre_name` are still protected with `UNIQUE` constraints, so the real\-world uniqueness they represent is enforced without making them load\-bearing identifiers.

## ON DELETE Behaviors

Four foreign keys each received a deliberate `ON DELETE` behavior rather than the implicit default. `ratings.movie_id` and `movie_genres.movie_id` both cascade, because a rating or a genre tag has no independent meaning once the movie it describes is gone — deleting the movie should clean up both automatically rather than leaving orphaned rows behind. `movie_genres.genre_id`, by contrast, restricts deletion: a genre is a shared category referenced by many movies, so removing one should be a deliberate, visible action, not something that silently strips a tag off the entire catalog. `ratings.user_id` was left at `NO ACTION`, which in practice rarely triggers, since users are never hard\-deleted in the first place — the `is_active` flag handles removal instead.

## Rules Enforced in the Schema vs. Left to the Application

The schema enforces structural integrity: primary keys guarantee uniqueness, `NOT NULL` constraints require the data every row genuinely needs, `UNIQUE` constraints protect `email` and `genre_name`, and foreign keys with their `ON DELETE` rules keep relationships consistent even under deletion. What the schema deliberately does not enforce is business logic that depends on interpretation or is likely to change: the valid range for `ratings.score` (1–5 versus 1–10, for example) was left to the application layer rather than hard\-coded as a `CHECK` constraint, since the scale hadn't been decided and may evolve. Similarly, cascading a user's `is_active = false` status into related records, validating email format, and enforcing that an active movie must have at least one genre are treated as application concerns — rules that involve workflow or judgment rather than raw data consistency, and that are cheaper to adjust in code than in a constraint.

## Reflection

The requirements left several structural decisions open rather than dictating them, and the clearest example is how to handle removing a user. Nothing in the assignment specified whether a user record should be permanently deleted or merely deactivated, so this was a judgment call rather than a fixed requirement.

A different designer could reasonably have chosen a hard delete instead — simply running `DELETE FROM users` when an account closes, letting `ON DELETE CASCADE` clean up their ratings automatically. That approach is simpler and keeps the table free of inactive rows.

I chose differently. Rather than deleting a user, `users` has an `is_active` flag, defaulting to `true`, and removing an account means flipping it to `false` instead of deleting the row. A user is not disposable the way a rating or genre tag is — someone might leave the platform and later return, and a hard delete would permanently erase their rating history along with them, even though that history stays meaningful. Soft\-deleting preserves it, lets a returning user pick up where they left off, and avoids the irreversible risk of deleting an account that should have been recoverable. Also this allows to keep the ratings that the user already created.
